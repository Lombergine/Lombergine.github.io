The International Canaries

Aditya Garg
September 2026 · draft, comments welcome · replication code

Abstract

Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen (2026) document a 19% relative employment decline for workers aged 22 to 25 in AI-exposed occupations, identified from US payroll microdata. I ask whether the same signal is recoverable across countries using public data, assembling a panel of 26 high-income countries observed 2019 to 2025. It is not, and the reason is instructive. Cumulative private AI investment per capita, the natural treatment variable, produces a positive slope that reverses sign when three southern European countries are excluded. An indicator for a native-English labour market, motivated by the fact that current language models are most capable in English, performs considerably better: the coefficient is +1.41 percentage points and retains its sign across every specification, falling to +0.76 with the 2020 unemployment shock, the pre-treatment trend and investment all controlled for. It is nonetheless never distinguishable from zero, and three defensible tests of the same contrast return p = 0.137, 0.053 and 0.043 depending on the variance assumption. A dynamic event study is more damaging still: the parallel-trends assumption fails. The 2020 pre-treatment coefficient is +1.82 (p = 0.059), larger than any post-treatment coefficient, so the post-2022 rise is the recovery limb of a pandemic V rather than a break from a stable baseline, and broader macro-cyclical volatility in these labour markets explains the path at least as well as any AI mechanism. Exact randomization inference over all 230,230 possible six-country groupings places the observed grouping at the 94.7th percentile (two-sided p = 0.139), confirming the classical test rather than rescuing it.

The central result is a power calculation. The minimum detectable effect at 80% power is 1.91 to 2.65 percentage points against a point estimate of 1.41, and recovering an effect of the observed magnitude would require approximately 43 comparable countries. Fewer exist. The cross-country design is therefore underpowered by construction rather than by sample selection, and no choice of treatment variable repairs it. Identification of this effect requires within-country variation at occupation-by-age resolution.

1Motivation

The Canaries result is identified from ADP payroll records covering roughly 4.6 million US workers across more than 730 occupations, crossing occupation-level AI exposure with worker age at monthly frequency. The design is powerful precisely because all of that variation sits inside one country.

That construction also makes the finding a US finding. Whether the entry-level effect generalises is an open and consequential question, and the obvious way to attack it is a cross-country panel. This paper asks whether that attack can work at all, using the public data actually available to someone outside a payroll provider.

The answer is no, and I think the negative result is worth stating precisely, because the arithmetic that defeats the design does not depend on the particular data used here.

2Data and measurement

The outcome is the youth employment penalty: a country's youth (15–24) unemployment rate minus its all-ages rate, in percentage points. Differencing against the all-ages rate within the same country and year removes country fixed effects and common macroeconomic shocks, leaving the age gradient the Canaries design targets.

3Investment-based exposure

The natural first specification regresses the change in the youth penalty on log cumulative AI investment per capita. Figure 1 plots it.

Excluding three of twenty-six observations moves the slope from +0.49 to −0.02 percentage points per log-dollar. The three countries in question recorded the largest post-pandemic unemployment declines in the sample for reasons unconnected to AI, so the full-sample slope is recovering southern European labour market recovery.

The deeper problem is conceptual. Investment per capita records where AI capital is booked, not where labour is exposed to it. A US firm's expenditure enters the US observation regardless of which country's work its models ultimately substitute for. The measure is misassigned by construction.

The placebo is reassuring without being decisive. If anything the sign reverses across the treatment date, though a reversal is also what mean reversion would produce, and Table 2 column (2) shows the 2020 shock does not itself explain the outcome.

5Dynamic response and the pre-treatment problem

Section 4's placebo compares two windows. A referee will reasonably ask for the full path, so Figure 3 plots year-by-year coefficients on English relative to the 2022 baseline. If the mechanism were AI, the pre-2022 coefficients should sit flat at zero and the series should break upward after ChatGPT.

It does not. The 2020 coefficient is +1.82 (p = 0.059), larger than any post-treatment coefficient and closer to conventional significance than any of them. The series traces a V: English-speaking labour markets spiked in 2020, converged by 2021 and 2022, then rose again through 2025. The post-2022 increase is the recovery limb of that V, not a departure from a flat baseline.

The parsimonious reading of the whole path is that these six labour markets are simply more cyclically volatile in both directions, which their weaker employment protection would predict. That is a mundane explanation and it fits the data at least as well as a language mechanism. This is the strongest single piece of evidence in the paper against the interpretation of Section 4, and it came from a test built to support it.

6Randomization inference

Sections 4 and 5 test one grouping of six countries. The natural objection is whether that grouping is special at all, or whether any six countries drawn from this sample would produce a difference of similar size. With n = 26 and six treated units the permutation distribution contains C(26,6) = 230,230 elements, few enough to enumerate exhaustively, so the randomization p-value here is exact rather than simulated.

The observed grouping sits at the 94.7th percentile: one arbitrary grouping of six countries in roughly nineteen produces a larger difference. The exact two-sided p is 0.139, which lands almost exactly on the classical t-test value of 0.137 in Table 3. That agreement matters. It means the parametric test was not misleading here, and the failure to reject is a property of the data rather than an artefact of the normality assumption. Run on the pre-treatment window the same exact test gives p = 0.198.

This is the strongest available answer to the identification worry raised in the next section, and it is worth being clear about its limits. Randomization inference establishes that the English grouping is unusual relative to arbitrary groupings and that no single member drives it. It cannot establish why the grouping is unusual, because every alternative explanation, language included, applies to the same six countries.

The disagreement is driven entirely by that dispersion gap: the treated group is roughly half as variable as the control group, so whether one imposes equal variances determines the verdict. When the assumption rather than the data decides the answer, the answer is not established. Reporting only the robust standard error, which is the specification that clears 5%, would be selecting a result.

A second identification problem is not fixable by estimation. All six treated countries are Anglo liberal-market economies with comparatively weak employment protection and flexible youth hiring. English is collinear with an entire institutional family, and six treated units cannot separate them. The coefficient in Table 2 should be read as an upper bound on a language effect, not an estimate of one.

8Statistical power

The preceding sections describe a design that cannot reject. It is worth asking what it could ever have rejected.

The minimum detectable effect exceeds the point estimate under either variance assumption. Holding the treated share and the observed within-group dispersion fixed, detecting a 1.41 percentage point effect at conventional power would require roughly 43 countries. The set of high-income economies publishing comparable labour force statistics is smaller than that.

This is the paper's substantive contribution, and it is a statement about the design rather than about artificial intelligence. The study is not underpowered because of an unlucky sample, a coarse outcome, or a poorly chosen treatment variable. It is underpowered because the cross-country panel has too few units and too little within-group variation for an effect of this magnitude, and no reweighting, no better exposure measure, and no additional country repairs that. Any cross-country study of the entry-level AI effect faces the same arithmetic.

9Limitations

  • English is confounded with institutions. Six treated units, all Anglo liberal-market economies. The design cannot separate language from employment protection, and I do not claim it does.
  • The pre-treatment path is not flat. Figure 3 shows a larger English coefficient in 2020 than in any post-treatment year. Cyclical volatility in these labour markets is a sufficient explanation for the whole series, and this design cannot rule it out.
  • Three post-treatment observations. Dropping the 2025 nowcast leaves the estimate unchanged (Table 4), so smoothing is not the issue, but the window is short regardless.
  • The outcome is too coarse. One observation per country-year averages away the occupation-by-age variation the effect is defined on.
  • Modelled ILO estimates are smoothed, and the direction of that bias is not obvious. These series shrink country-year observations toward a model fit, which attenuates genuine country-specific deviations, so +1.41 is plausibly an understatement of any true effect. But the same shrinkage reduces residual variance, so the standard error is understated in step. The net effect on power is therefore ambiguous, and claiming smoothing mechanically inflates standard errors, as is sometimes asserted, has the sign backwards: less residual variance means smaller standard errors, not larger. Neither direction rescues the design, because the sample size binds regardless.
  • The dose-response test fails. Ranking the seventeen non-native European countries by EF English Proficiency Index gives ρ = +0.12 (p = 0.65) against the post-2022 change, and a larger ρ = +0.46 against the placebo window. A mechanism linear in proficiency should have appeared here and did not, which is evidence against the language interpretation. Sources disagree on EF levels, so only ranks were used.

10What would settle it

The power calculation implies that adding countries is not the fix. Adding resolution within countries is:

  1. Occupation-by-age employment counts from EU-LFS or national labour force surveys at quarterly frequency, crossed with an ISCO-mapped AI exposure measure. This relocates the identifying variation inside countries, where there is enough of it, and reproduces the Canaries design outside the United States. It is tractable with public EU-LFS microdata access and is the extension I would build next.
  2. Exposure defined on tasks rather than nationality. The share of a country's employment in occupations whose output is English-language, which would separate language from the Anglo institutional bundle that defeats Section 7.
  3. Payroll microdata outside the United States. The reason the original design works is ADP. No public equivalent exists, and that remains the binding constraint.
