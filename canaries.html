Replication attempt · public data · September 2026

The International Canaries

Stanford's Digital Economy Lab finds a 19% employment shortfall for 22 to 25 year olds in AI-exposed occupations, using US payroll microdata. I tried to find the same signal across 26 countries with public data. This is what I found instead.

Aditya Garg · 182 country-years, World Bank / ILO · code and data
The finding

The cross-country design cannot answer this question, and the reason is arithmetic rather than data quality. Detecting an effect the size of the one visible here would take about 43 comparable countries. There are not 43.

Two things follow from that. The AI-investment measure everyone reaches for first is the wrong treatment variable and behaves like it. A language-exposure measure behaves considerably better and holds its sign under every control. Neither can be established at n=26, and no reweighting of the same 26 countries fixes it.

Step one

The obvious treatment variable fails

The natural first attempt: regress the change in each country's youth employment penalty since 2022 on cumulative private AI investment per capita. The penalty is the youth (15-24) unemployment rate minus the all-ages rate; 2022 is the pre-ChatGPT baseline.

A relationship that reverses sign on the removal of 12% of the sample is not a finding. But the more useful diagnosis is why it fails: investment per capita measures where AI capital is booked, not where labour is exposed to it. A US firm's capital appears in the US line whatever country's work its models displace.

Step two

A treatment variable with a mechanism behind it

If large language models substitute for entry-level cognitive work, they should substitute first where they are most capable, and current models are most capable in English. That predicts exposure tracks the language of the labour market, not the location of the capital. It is a sharper prediction, and unlike investment it is measured on the side of the market where the displacement happens.

The test: an indicator for a native-English labour market (United States, United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, Ireland), against the same outcome, under progressively harsher controls.

Controlling for the size of each country's 2020 youth unemployment shock, its pre-ChatGPT trend, and AI investment per capita together moves the estimate from +1.41 to +0.76 percentage points. It never changes sign. The placebo, running the same split on the pre-ChatGPT window, gives a difference of −0.90pp at p = 0.11, so the pattern is not simply a continuation of how these labour markets already differed.

Step three

Where the honest reader should stop

The same contrast, tested three defensible ways, does not agree with itself:

Test of the same contrasttdfpReject at 5%?

The disagreement comes from dispersion: the six English-speaking countries have a standard deviation of 1.16 percentage points, the other twenty have 2.12. Whether you assume equal variances decides the answer, which means the answer is not being decided by the data. Anyone reporting only the robust standard error here would be choosing their result.

There is a second problem no amount of estimation fixes. All six treated countries are Anglo liberal-market economies with weak employment protection and flexible youth hiring. English is perfectly confounded with an entire institutional family, and n=6 cannot separate them.

Step four

What this design could ever have detected

This is the result worth carrying away. The study is not underpowered because of a bad choice of countries or a coarse outcome; it is underpowered by construction. There are not 43 high-income countries with comparable labour force statistics, so no cross-country panel of this shape can resolve an effect of this magnitude, whatever treatment variable it uses. That is a fact about the design, not about AI.

Method

What was computed

  • Outcome. Youth employment penalty = youth (15-24) unemployment rate minus the all-ages rate, per country per year, differenced 2022 to 2025. World Bank SL.UEM.1524.ZS and SL.UEM.TOTL.ZS, modelled ILO estimates. 2025 values are nowcasts, not observed.
  • Treatments. (i) Cumulative private AI investment 2013-2024 from the Stanford AI Index over 2024 population, in logs. (ii) A native-English labour market indicator.
  • Specifications. OLS with classical and HC1 robust standard errors, controlling in turn for the 2020 youth unemployment shock, the 2019-2022 pre-trend, and log investment per capita. Welch and pooled two-sample t-tests on the same contrast. A placebo on the pre-ChatGPT window.
  • Power. Minimum detectable effect at 80% power and 5% two-sided, from the realised standard error; required sample from the pooled within-group variance holding the treated share fixed.
  • Sample. 26 OECD and high-income countries with continuous 2019-2025 coverage. Five fall below the AI Index reporting threshold of roughly $1B and are coded at $0.5B, ringed in Figure 1.
Limitations

What would make this wrong

  • English is confounded with institutions. All six treated countries are Anglo liberal-market economies. This design cannot tell language apart from employment protection, and I do not claim it can.
  • Three post-period observations, one of which is a nowcast rather than an observed rate.
  • The outcome is far too coarse. Canaries identifies off occupation-level exposure crossed with age inside one country, monthly. One number per country per year averages away the variation the effect lives in.
  • Modelled ILO estimates are smoothed and are not national-definition unemployment rates. They are comparable across countries at the cost of being less sensitive within them.
  • A within-non-native dose-response test failed. Ranking the seventeen non-native European countries by EF English Proficiency Index gives ρ = +0.12 (p = 0.65) against the post-2022 change, and a larger ρ = +0.46 against the placebo window. If the mechanism were linear in proficiency it should have shown up here, and it does not. Sources also disagree on EF levels, so only ranks were used.
Next

What would settle it

Given the power calculation, more countries is not the answer. More resolution per country is:

  • Occupation-by-age employment counts from EU-LFS or national labour force surveys, quarterly, crossed with an ISCO-mapped AI exposure measure. This moves the identifying variation inside countries, where there is enough of it, and reproduces the Canaries design outside the US.
  • Language exposure measured on tasks, not nationality. The share of a country's employment in occupations whose output is English-language, which separates language from the Anglo institutional bundle that defeats the test above.
  • Payroll microdata outside the US. The reason Canaries works is ADP. There is no public equivalent, and that is the real bottleneck.

The first is tractable with public EU-LFS microdata access and is what I would build next.
